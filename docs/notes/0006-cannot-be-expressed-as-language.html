In Progress - The corpus of knowledge that cannot be expressed as language

There is knowledge that cannot be expressed as language. This page is a text attempt at that corpus, which is impossible: text is the one modality the corpus is about what escapes. The only move is to use language to point at where language gives out, and to be honest that the pointing is not the thing.

1. You can recite "push off, pedal, balance" until you are blue. The first time you ride a bike without training wheels is a fall that does not happen, and the not-happening is in the legs, not the words.

2. Salt on the tongue. The taste is there and no sentence has carried it.

3. "Left over right, make a loop, wrap around, pull through." Say the shoelace rhyme a thousand times. The knot still has to be found by the fingers, in a motion no diagram completes.

4. My mother could dice an onion in forty seconds without looking at the board. The knife knew where the root was, and her wrist knew the weight of the blade, and I watched for years and never learned it from watching.

5. The pool where I learned to swim had a shallow end that dropped to eight feet at a painted line. You cross the line and the water takes your feet, and then one day it does not, and the day it does not is not a day you can be told about.

6. Close your eyes. You know where your left index finger is, and you did not work it out.

7. The surgeon calls it "tissue feel." A gallbladder that is angry is stiff under the glove in a way the screen never shows, and the resident learns it by pressing, not by reading.

8. Clay on the wheel. The potter centers it with both palms and the wobble goes out through the hands, and the going-out is a vibration that quiets under the skin. No instruction transfers the stillness.

9. A spruce top sounds different from cedar under the luthier's knuckle. He taps it once and hears the resonance, and the hearing is a judgment the ear makes that no spec sheet records.

10. You tilt your head and the room tilts with it, except it does not. The inner ear gives the vertical, and the giving has no sentence.

11. "I have a migraine." The sentence is calm. The thing itself is a sick light behind the left eye that makes you lie still in a dark room and wait, and no description has ever passed it along to someone who has not had one.

12. The red of a stop sign at dusk is not the red of a stop sign at noon, and neither is the red of the inside of your eyelid when you face the sun. The word red covers all three and carries none.

13. Rain in Tucson smells different from rain in Vermont. Creosote and hot asphalt, wet pine and cold dirt. The nose knows, and the nose does not write.

14. You are hungry. Not the idea of hunger. The hollow ache under the sternum that makes the fridge seem important, that no sentence has ever reproduced in a fed person.

15. The first time I walked home from school alone I did not look at a map. The route was in my legs: past the pharmacy, left at the broken sidewalk, cut through the Hendersons' yard. I could not have written it down correctly, but my feet got me there.

16. The cellist draws the bow and the room fills with a low tone you feel in the ribs before you hear it in the ears. The sound has a weight, and the weight is the thing, and the word cello does not have it.

17. Nausea is the body insisting. It is a rolling in the stomach that puts a sweat on the forehead, and you can describe it to a doctor and the doctor will nod, and the nod does not have the rolling in it.

18. A cold morning, minus twelve, walking to the bus stop without gloves. The knuckles ache first, then go numb, then burn. The burning is the cold, and you cannot teach it to someone who lives where it does not freeze.

19. Stand on one foot. The corrections are constant and tiny and you are not making them on purpose. Balance is a conversation the ankle has with the ground that the head is not party to.

20. The old carpenter runs his thumb along a board's edge and shakes his head. Tearout. He felt it before he saw it, and the feeling is a judgment the skin makes in half a second that no manual teaches.

21. You catch a glass that slips off the counter. Your hand is there before you decide to move it, and the deciding happened somewhere the language does not reach.

22. The auditorium where I first played violin had a two-second decay. You could hear your own note come back at you from the back wall, and after a year in that room I could feel, from the stage, whether the seats were full or empty by the way the air gave back sound.

23. The stitch in your side at mile four. A sharp cramp under the ribs that tells you to slow down, and the telling is a pain, and the pain is the instruction, and no coach's sentence is the instruction.

24. Your own kitchen in a blackout. You reach for the coffee tin in the dark and your hand goes straight to it. The room is mapped in your arm, not in any drawing.

25. The woodworker cups a plank end and listens. A dry ring means ready; a dull thud means wait. The ear decides, and the ear's decision has no sentence behind it.

26. The feel of being tilted: sitting in a small plane banking left, the g-force pushing you into the seat at an angle that is neither lying nor sitting, and your body reads it as a slope before your eyes confirm the horizon has rolled.

27. My grandmother's house had thirteen stairs from the first floor to the second, and I knew the thirteenth was an inch shorter than the rest. My foot knew it. I could not have told a visitor without miscounting.

28. The first time you steer a car through a skid you turn into it, and the instruction is right but the doing is a commitment the hands make that the brain has to follow. The brain is a passenger in that moment.

29. Walking in your own house at night with no lights. You know the doorway is there before your hand finds the frame, and the knowing is a distance the body carries that no map shows.

30. The ophthalmologist says "red" and holds up a card. The red on the card is a red you and she both call red, and the red in your head when you close your eyes is the same word, different thing. The word is shared; the thing is private, and no sentence has moved it from one head to another.

31. The week my mother died I sat at the kitchen table and wrote "I am sad she died" in a notebook. The sentence sat there doing nothing while my chest did something no line of mine has ever carried.

32. Anger is heat in the face and a clenching in the jaw, and I learned what it was the afternoon my brother broke my guitar and I could not make any sentence hold the want to hit him.

33. After I cried for an hour in the hospital bathroom I came out flat, emptied, almost calm. No adjective in the language reproduces that post-crying flatness for someone who has not stood up from a toilet lid with wet sleeves.

34. Joy, the real kind, is the second my daughter took her first steps across the living room rug and I made a sound I had never made before. I cannot get that sound back by describing it.

35. Sadness is not crying. Sadness is the three days after the funeral when I kept forgetting and then remembering, and each remembering was a small drop in the stomach that no grief manual conveys.

36. She wakes already knowing which child is crying, before any word forms. No manual of parenting transmits that instantaneous bodily identification.

37. Missing a person is a dull pull in the ribs that arrives without an object. The phrase "I miss you" does not move the ribs.

38. My son fell asleep holding my index finger and his grip did not loosen for an hour. There is no sentence that holds the quality of that grip.

39. I saw her across the food court before she saw me and my feet began moving. No account of attraction describes the feet beginning to move on their own.

40. Loving my mother in her last year was a daily act of dressing a wound I could not name. The word "love" did not cover the dressing.

41. The sky over the desert at 2 a.m. with no moon, no light pollution, the Milky Way a smear across the whole of it. I stood there until my neck hurt and no photograph or astronomy text has ever given me back the smallness.

42. From the plane the Cascades were a row of white teeth and I pressed my face to the cold window until my breath fogged. The scale of them refused to fit inside my head, and still refuses.

43. The first chord of the organ in St. Mark's hit my sternum before my ears registered it. I do not know a way to write that physical bass so a reader feels it in their own chest.

44. Before the thunderstorm the air went white and still and the birds stopped. That hush is a held breath in the body of everyone standing in it, and weather reports do not carry it.

45. The Grand Canyon at sunset made me forget I had legs for a full minute. No travel writer has yet given me that forgetting.

46. The night before the biopsy I lay awake rehearsing the doctor's face. The dread was a cold ball low in the belly, and the phrase "I am worried about my test results" is a clean lie next to it.

47. The truck ran the red and I braked and stopped two feet short. For thirty seconds my hands shook and my hearing went thin, and no near-miss report makes the reader's hands shake.

48. 3 a.m. The mind finds the one unfinished worry and runs it like a tongue over a broken tooth. No symptom list gives the loop its actual rhythm.

49. The door locked from the outside and the lights off and I was nine. The panic was a thing in my throat that did not let me scream, and I have never found the sentence for it.

50. I learned what dread was in the waiting room before the oncologist called my father's name. The chair's vinyl was cold under my palms and the clock ticked and the knowing was in the cold palms, not in any word I had.

51. The house I grew up in was torn down for a parking lot in 2009. I drove past the lot last summer and the ache was specific to that flattened rectangle, not to any general idea of loss.

52. Homesickness at college was a wanting of the smell of my father's coffee at 6 a.m. No letter home transmitted that smell.

53. The smell of cut grass in May returned my grandmother's yard whole, the slope of it, the rope swing. The yard is gone but the smell carries it, and no prose of mine does.

54. I keep the last voicemail my father left, from a Tuesday, about nothing. The want to hear him alive in that recording is a pull no written elegy performs.

55. There was a dirt path behind the elementary school, now a strip mall. Standing in the parking lot I could feel where the path had been in my feet, a ghost no city map renders.

56. I was caught lying to my wife about the money and the heat climbed up my neck into my ears. The phrase "I felt ashamed" reports the fact and omits the heat entirely.

57. I said the thing that made my friend go quiet, and in the quiet my stomach dropped like a stone. The sentence "I realized I had hurt him" is a summary that leaves out the drop.

58. At twelve I shoplifted a candy bar and the clerk saw me. The wish to vanish was physical, a pulling inward of the whole body, and no confession I have written has reproduced it.

59. Shame is the want for the floor to open. I felt it at the dinner table when my mother repeated my secret in front of my brother, and no memoir paragraph has given me back the floor's refusal to open.

60. The morning after I sent the cruel email I read it again and my face went hot and my eyes dropped to the desk. The hot face and the dropped eyes are the knowledge, and "I felt guilty" is only its caption.

61. At Wijk aan Zee in 1999, Kasparov sacrificed a rook on move 24 against Topalov and said afterward he saw the winning line before calculating a single variation. The score sheet records the moves. It does not record the recognition that produced them.

62. You sit beside a violist in a community orchestra run-through and she flinches at a note the conductor missed and the tuner did not flag. Her fingers knew the intonation was flat before her ear could name it.

63. Ted Williams claimed he could read the seams on the ball and tell a fastball from a curve in the first ten feet of flight. A hitter has roughly a quarter second to commit. The decision rides on a read no batting manual has put into words.

64. "I knew it by the jizz." Roger Tory Peterson identified birds from a half-second silhouette against gray sky by shape and wingbeat rhythm, and the birders who learned from his guides used a slang word for the thing the guides could not contain.

65. The cognitive psychologist Gary Klein tells the story of a fireground commander who ordered his crew out of a house seconds before the floor collapsed, and could say only that the fire felt "too quiet." Fifteen years of fires had assembled a pattern he had no words for.

66. A pediatric ER nurse told a researcher she flagged a toddler as septic before the vitals came back, because the child "didn't cry right." The sepsis protocol has five criteria. She used none of them. She was right.

67. The mechanic on Fourth Street listens to your engine for four seconds and says the third cylinder is missing. The diagnostic computer takes twenty minutes to agree.

68. At the 1976 Judgment of Paris, Steven Spurrier's panel of French experts could not tell California cabernet from Bordeaux first-growth when the bottles were covered. Given labels, the same palates were certain. Given only the wine, they were guessing.

69. An ER attending in Baltimore sent a chest-pain patient to the cath lab with a normal EKG and normal enzymes because the man "looked like he was dying," and the angiogram showed a 95% blockage in the left anterior descending. Residents ask him for the rule. There is no rule.

70. A violin maker taps a spruce top with his knuckle and rejects three boards out of four. He has graded wood for forty years and cannot describe a good tap. He can only tell you, after, which ones were not good.

71. You walk into the kitchen at Thanksgiving and your mother and your sister stop talking at the same instant. Nobody left. Nobody raised a voice. You know there was a fight.

72. On the 1962 Oval Office tapes, you can hear the room turn against the airstrike during the Cuban Missile Crisis without any vote being taken. Kennedy reads the shift and the policy follows.

73. A substitute teacher walks into a classroom and knows within ten seconds whether the class will eat her alive, from the way the fifth-row boy looked at the second-row girl when the door opened. She has no data. She has the look.

74. The deck is flawless, the numbers are strong, and the room is dead. The founder leaves the pitch meeting baffled by the pass. The partners passed because nobody leaned forward.

75. At a wedding reception the dance floor fills or it doesn't, and the band has its verdict by the second song. A packed floor and an empty one look identical in the contract.

76. You meet someone at a conference and within ninety seconds you have decided you would lend them your car. Ask why and you will invent reasons. The decision was made below the reasons.

77. A polygraph examiner with thirty years of interviews told a reporter that the machine is theatre. He knows someone is lying by the way their shoulders sit when they say no. The machine is for the record. He already had the answer.

78. There is a man standing at the edge of the playground who has done nothing, and three mothers have already moved between him and the strollers. They will not be able to file a complaint. They were not wrong.

79. You can tell in the first minute of a job interview that the candidate is performing confidence rather than having it: the performed version is louder and more still at the same time.

80. Bill Clinton could make a person in a rope line feel like the only person in the room, and reporters kept trying to describe how and failing. He was not paying more attention than anyone else. He was paying a different kind.

81. A high school math teacher in Vermont told a researcher she calls on a student when his pencil stops, not when his hand goes up. The hand means he has the answer. The pencil means he is close.

82. You are explaining integration by parts and the student nods and says "right, right," and the speed of the nod tells you he has lost the thread. You re-explain. You do not know how you knew.

83. A cello teacher at Juilliard said the hardest call in her job is whether to push a struggling student or release him, and she makes it by how he holds the bow in the silence after a mistake.

84. A kindergarten teacher names which of her kids skipped breakfast and is right four times in five. She does not check lunchboxes. She reads the walk to the cubby.

85. A surgery professor said he knew a resident would be good when the resident stepped back from the table at the right moment without being told. He could teach the steps. He could not teach the stepping back.

86. The Grateful Dead's 1977 Cornell show opened with "New Minglewood Blues" and the band later said the first four bars told them the crowd would give them everything. A band locks in a way no member initiates.

87. An MIT study put small teams in a room to solve a hard problem and found the winners were not the teams with the smartest members but the teams whose members took turns talking. The group had a property none of the individuals carried.

88. A forty-voice gospel choir finds a blend no singer chooses. A trained ear hears the moment one voice pushes its own tone. The blend is a decision made below any singer's intention.

89. A basketball team on a run plays faster than any player decides to play. The coach calls timeout to stop the other team's momentum and his own team's run in the same whistle. The flow belonged to the group.

90. A crowd at a farmer's market sets the fair tomato price within the first hour, and no individual set it. The vendor who holds above the crowd price goes home with crates.

91. You sit in the Quaker meeting-house on a Sunday in Lancaster and for an hour no one speaks, and the silence is not empty but full, and a visitor who asks "what just happened" has already missed it. The shared silence is the practice, and no one explained it.

92. On a Tokyo subway car at rush hour, two hundred people stand packed and silent, and a foreigner who takes a phone call feels the whole car turn cold without anyone looking. The culture runs on a silence everyone reads and no sign announces.

93. At my grandmother's kitchen table in County Cork, no one said "I love you." You knew it because she put the last potato on your plate and took none for herself.

94. In the village near Aleppo where my father grew up, a guest who praised an object in the house received it as a gift, and so you learned never to admire anything you did not want to take home. The rule was never posted; a child who violated it was corrected once and never forgot.

95. The American Southerner who says "bless your heart" is not blessing you, and every Southerner knows this, and no outsider's dictionary carries the contempt.

96. You do not ask the widow, at the wake, whether she will remarry. The room itself enforces the line without a word.

97. In the Hindu temple at Tirupati, the inner sanctum is dark and you do not look at the deity's face until the curtain lifts, and the priest's hand on your shoulder corrects you if you try. The prohibition lives in the hand.

98. The friend's husband is drunk and cruel at the dinner, and you say nothing, and the nothing you say is the agreement the whole table has made to keep the marriage intact past tonight.

99. My mother kept a box of photographs of her first husband and I never opened it, not because she forbade me but because the box was hers in a way that made my hand stop. Some property is sacred and the sacred is known by the hand that stops.

100. On the night my uncle died, my aunt said "we had a good run" and laughed, and no one corrected her, because grief at that volume has its own rules about what can be said back.

101. You do not congratulate a pregnant woman before the third month. The superstition is gone but the silence around the first trimester remains, because too many women have bled in the twelfth week and then had to explain.

102. The first chord of the Passacaglia hits the back of the throat before the ear, and a musician who tries to explain why it moves you runs out of words at "it just does." The beauty is in the resonance and the resonance is bodily.

103. A face is beautiful and the sentence "her cheekbones were high" describes none of it. The beauty was a proportion the face held in motion, and a still photograph of it loses the thing that stopped you.

104. The last line of the Hopkins sonnet, the one that goes "mine, O mine," closes a sound that has been building for thirteen lines, and the closure is a physical event in the mouth that has read it aloud. No description of the closure produces it in a reader who has not spoken the poem.

105. My father could tell when a wall was plumb by leaning his head against it and listening, and the mason he trained under in the seventies called this "the ear for true." A level tells you the wall is vertical; the ear tells you the wall is right.

106. Bill Evans plays the opening chords of "Waltz for Debby" and the room where I first heard it in 1998 becomes the room I am always trying to get back to. The beauty is a place the music made, and no analysis of the voicings returns me to it.

107. The yuck. A man tells you he has been sleeping with his adult stepdaughter and your stomach turns before you have a principle to name. The principle comes later, dressed up to justify the turn; the turn was the knowledge.

108. I was eleven and I stole a comic book from Mr. Reilly's shop, and for three days the weight in my chest was heavier than the book, and the weight did not lift until I walked back in and put it on the counter. No catechism produced that weight.

109. A factory closes and three hundred people lose their work, and the CEO who signed the closure feels nothing he can name, and the line worker who lost her shift feels a wrong that no economist's column will call wrong.

110. You watch a man strike a dog in the street and your hand is at his collar before your reason has formed a sentence. The moral knowledge moved faster than thought, and thought arrives to write the report.

111. The child who sees another child beaten on the playground and looks away has committed something, and the looking away is not neutral. The neutral is a fiction adults invented to make the looking away bearable.

112. The summer I was nineteen I sat in the chapel at Taizé and could not name what had gone quiet in me, and the quiet was a presence, not an absence, and I have spent twenty years trying to find it again. The mystics call it contemplation and the word is a stub for the event.

113. My grandfather prayed the rosary every morning at five and I asked him once what he said to God, and he looked at me as if I had asked him to describe breathing. The prayer was the shape of the morning, and speech had nothing to do with it.

114. In the zendo at Tassajara at four in the morning, the bell rings and two hundred people sit and the silence becomes a thing with weight, and a newcomer who expected peace finds instead a sharpness that is almost unbearable. The numinous does not soothe. The numinous is the ceiling removed.

115. The Hasidic story of the boy who forgot how to play the flute and could only sing, and could not even sing, and could only say the word "flute," and that word was what God heard. The knowledge is that the broken offering is the offering, and no theology states it as plainly as the story.

116. On the night my mother died, the hospice nurse said "she is gone" and I felt the room change, and the change was a thinning, as if a layer between two airs had opened. No framework of the afterlife names what I felt in that room.

117. The proof that the square root of two is irrational has a move in it, the descent, and you either see the move or you do not, and the seeing is not the reading of the steps. I read the proof at fifteen and did not see it, and read it again at sixteen and saw it, and nothing between the two readings had changed but me.

118. A mathematician I knew could feel when a conjecture was true before she had the proof, and called it "the weight of the structure." The proof was the paperwork; the weight was the knowledge.

119. The reason every prime greater than two is odd is trivial once stated, and a child who has not seen it cannot be argued into it, only shown it and left to sit with it until it lands. The landing is the understanding, and it happens in a place argument does not reach.

120. Ramanujan said the goddess wrote the formulas on his tongue in sleep, and Hardy, who was the greatest formalist of his age, could not produce what Ramanujan produced asleep. The structure was given to one and worked for by the other, and the difference between given and worked is the difference that no methodology closes.
